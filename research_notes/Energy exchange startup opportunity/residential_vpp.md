## Key Question 1: What does a household actually earn per year from a VPP in the US, UK, Germany and Australia, and what does the aggregator keep?

### Takeaway
Realised household VPP income is modest everywhere: roughly USD 100-300/yr per battery in the big US programs (up to ~USD 800-1,000 in the best-paying Northeast capacity programs), ~GBP 5/household/yr from UK turn-down sessions, AUD 130-600/yr in Australian retailer VPPs (with Amber's wholesale pass-through as the high-variance outlier), and in Germany the value shows up mostly as dynamic-tariff bill savings rather than cash payouts. Aggregator take rates are rarely disclosed; the visible models are a fixed per-battery payout with the aggregator keeping the residual (Sunrun), a flat subscription with 100% pass-through (Amber, 1KOMMA5°), or revenue-share "bonuses" (sonnen).

### Inferences
- Across all four markets the aggregator's cash payout to a battery household clusters at ~USD/AUD 130-300 per year unless the household sits in a capacity-constrained market with a USD/kW capacity payment (MA/CT ConnectedSolutions) or takes direct wholesale price risk (Amber). The "USD 1,000/yr" figures are best-case, best-market numbers from secondary sources.
- Take rates are opaque. Sunrun's structure (fixed USD 150/battery to the customer, residual to Sunrun) and Amber's (AUD 300/yr subscription, 100% pass-through) are the two transparent extremes; neither party discloses what the aggregator nets per battery.
- For a 3 kW PM Surya Ghar household in India with no battery, the benchmark implies annual flexibility value in the low hundreds of rupees unless there is a capacity-type payment — a critical design constraint for any Indian VPP.

### Gaps
- No primary-source disclosure of Tesla's or Sunrun's VPP revenue share per battery; Tesla's own ConnectedSolutions results page could not be fetched (host blocked).
- No measured per-household EUR payout for sonnen, Tibber or 1KOMMA5° in Germany; only vendor projections.
- No Intelligent Octopus Flux / Kraken Flex per-household earnings figure found.
- The ACCC "median AUD 55/quarter" figure was only seen via an Amber blog citing Energy Consumers Australia; the primary ACCC report was not retrieved.

## Key Question 2: Which revenue streams are the largest and most durable? Which have collapsed?

### Takeaway
Capacity / peak-demand-response payments (utility programs, RA/ELRP in California, ConnectedSolutions in New England, DFPO-type obligations) are the largest and most durable residential stream; frequency/ancillary services were lucrative early but saturate quickly (Australia FCAS, GB FFR/Dynamic services), and pure wholesale arbitrage is volatile and event-driven. Network deferral is still mostly aspirational outside targeted utility programs.

### Inferences
- Durable ranking for a residential battery fleet: (1) capacity/peak DR contracts with utilities or capacity markets; (2) retail tariff optimisation / self-consumption (which the household captures itself); (3) wholesale arbitrage via dynamic tariffs; (4) ancillary services — fastest to saturate because a few hundred MW of grid batteries can fill the whole market.
- Markets where VPP income is being sustained are shifting from "sell ancillary services" to "sell firm peak capacity to whoever is short" (utilities, PJM, now data centers), which requires accurate dispatch and measurement — hence the importance of smart metering/telemetry.

- No Dynamic Containment-specific GB price series for 2024-25 was retrieved.
- No quantified Australian FCAS price trend 2022→2025 for VPP-sized participants beyond the single AU$214/MW data point.
- Network-deferral payments to residential VPPs: no quantified program found in this research (only Brattle/RMI value estimates).

## Key Question 3: What is the minimum regulatory enabler for a residential VPP, and which exist in India today vs on the drawing board?

### Takeaway
A residential VPP needs (a) interval metering and settlement at the premise, (b) a legal counterparty that can be paid for flexibility (a DISCOM DR program, a retailer tariff, or a market-registered aggregator), and (c) a price signal (ToD tariff, capacity payment, or wholesale pass-through). India has (a) partially (5.7 crore RDSS smart meters, ~28% of target), (c) partially (ToD rules in force but residential alignment patchy), and (b) only at state level via DISCOM DSM/DF programs — no national aggregator licence, and no route for residential prosumers or aggregators to sell into IEX. Draft 2026 consumer-rights rules and state DF regulations (Maharashtra 2024, Rajasthan 2026, Karnataka/Assam drafts) are the drawing board.

### Inferences
- Today an Indian residential VPP can only be monetised as a DISCOM-contracted DR/DSM program (TPDDL-style), because (i) exchange access requires ≥100 kW open-access eligibility and IEX membership categories exclude households, and (ii) CERC ancillary markets require ≥1 MW blocks with SCADA telemetry and no defined aggregator role. The RERC 2026 aggregator registration is the first state-level legal "aggregator" status; Maharashtra's DFPO creates the first compulsory buyer.
- The DFPO model (MERC 2024 → RERC 2026 → KERC/AERC drafts) is the most likely national template; the 2026 draft Rule 17 would push every SERC to define DR provider eligibility — the hook on which an aggregator licence could hang.
- With ~5.5 crore consumer smart meters but residential ToD mostly misaligned or deferred, the near-term addressable base is Delhi, Maharashtra, Odisha, AP, Tamil Nadu — states with both smart meters and solar-aligned ToD.

## Key Question 4: Is residential rooftop export curtailment or net metering rollback in India creating a pain point a VPP can solve?

### Takeaway
Yes, but it is emerging rather than acute: PM Surya Ghar has added ~15 GW of residential rooftop in 2.5 years, and states are responding with feeder-level caps (70% in Maharashtra's 2026 draft), low surplus export tariffs (INR 2.82/kWh in Maharashtra FY27), storage mandates for larger prosumers, and a national shift of 12 states to net billing — all of which lower the value of midday export and raise the value of shifting/storing it. Residential net metering itself is still largely protected (and simplified by MoP's Oct 2025 push), so the pain point is "export worth less", not "export prohibited".

### Inferences
- The pain point is forming in exactly the sequence seen in Australia: fast residential PV growth → DT/feeder hosting caps → lower export tariffs/net billing → storage mandates → (eventually) battery subsidies and VPP enablement. - At INR 2.82/kWh export vs residential retail tariffs of roughly INR 6-8/kWh, every kWh a household can shift from export to self-consumption is worth ~INR 3-5 — the main economic value a HEMS/VPP can offer a PM Surya Ghar household today, ahead of any DISCOM DR payment.
- Residential battery penetration is tiny (national BESS stock ~1.1 GWh across all segments), so near-term Indian VPP capacity would have to come from load (ACs, water heaters, EV chargers) and inverter curtailment control rather than batteries.

## Key Question 5: What business models have failed and why?

### Takeaway

Common threads: program revenue arriving only after large upfront hardware/CAC spend, dependence on a few utility programs, and churn of 12%+ in behavioural programs.

### Inferences
- Models that survive own the customer relationship and the asset (Tesla, Sunrun, Octopus/Kraken, 1KOMMA5°, Amber as retailer) or sell software B2B to those who do (Kraken, Evergen, SwitchDin, EnergyHub, Uplight). Standalone third-party battery aggregators financing hardware against future program revenue (Swell) and standalone behavioural-DR apps (OhmConnect) did not survive independently.
- For India this argues for a retailer/DISCOM-embedded or installer-embedded (PM Surya Ghar vendor) model rather than a standalone consumer app.
